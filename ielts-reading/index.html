Reading Altitude

Students fail IELTS Reading on the clock, not on the vocabulary. This is a ten-station system for teaching them to change altitude on purpose — fly high to map a text, drop low to scan for one fact, and never read every word again.

Station 0 · 8 minutes

The Time Crisis

Never argue that students read too slowly — show them their own number. They read one real passage, the page measures their speed, and then does the exam arithmetic in front of them. This is the moment the rest of the lesson becomes worth their attention.

How to run it
Do this before teaching anything. Project it, have the whole class read together, and have every student press Stop when they finish — not when the fastest finishes. Write two or three of their WPM numbers on the board. Then reveal the projection bar. The silence you get is the lesson. Typical L2 reading speed at B1–B2 is roughly 90–150 wpm; native academic readers sit around 250–300.

0.0s

For most of human history, growing food has meant spreading outwards across the land. Vertical farming proposes the opposite: stacking crops in layers inside a controlled building, lit by tuned LED arrays and fed by recirculating water rather than soil. A single warehouse floor, repeated twelve times upwards, can in principle produce what several hectares of open field would yield in a season.

The appeal is easy to state. Because the growing environment is sealed, yields do not depend on the weather, and harvests can run continuously through winter. Water use falls dramatically, since most systems recover and reuse more than ninety per cent of what they circulate. Pesticides become largely unnecessary, because pests are excluded rather than killed. And because the farm can sit inside the city it supplies, the long refrigerated journey from field to shelf largely disappears.

The difficulty is electricity. Sunlight is free; LED light is not. Analysts estimate that lighting and climate control account for the majority of a vertical farm's running costs, which is why the sector has concentrated on crops that are light, fast-growing and expensive per kilogram: salad leaves, herbs and soft fruit. Wheat, rice and potatoes, the crops that actually feed populations, remain stubbornly uneconomic to grow this way.

Several high-profile operators collapsed when energy prices rose sharply, and the industry has since become noticeably more cautious in its claims. Researchers now tend to describe vertical farming not as a replacement for conventional agriculture but as a narrow supplement to it: valuable in dense cities, in extreme climates, and wherever fresh produce would otherwise have to travel too far.

Station 1 · 10 minutes

The Reading Altitude

Here the passage becomes a landscape. Every word is a block; every paragraph is a plateau. The only thing that changes between the three modes is how high the camera flies — and that single idea, altitude, is the whole of skimming and scanning. Fly high and you see the shape but not the words. Drop to ground level and you see every word but nothing else.

How to run it
Run Ground level first and let it get boring — that's the point; ask the class to shout when they get impatient. Then Cruising altitude: ask "can you read any of it? No. But can you see there are three regions? Yes." Then Attack run: "the map told us it's in the third region, so we drop only there." Finish by giving the class the phrase to reuse all lesson: "What altitude are you at right now?"

Station 2 · 12 minutes

The Six-Step Method

Same passage as the flight, now on the ground and slowed down. Six steps, one real True/False/Not Given question, and a moving dot showing precisely where a trained reader's eyes go — and, just as importantly, where they never go.

How to run it
Advance with → and ← on the keyboard so you can stand away from the laptop. Before pressing Next at step 4, make the class call out which words are the keywords. Before step 6, take a vote on TRUE or FALSE — most classes vote TRUE, which is exactly the trap. That wrong vote is worth more than any explanation you could give.
Speed
The question · True, False or Not Given?

Critics believe that adding more hives always increases wild bee populations.

Answer: FALSE — the passage states the opposite.

Words fixated: 0 / –

Beekeeping has quietly moved from the countryside into cities over the last two decades. In London alone, the number of registered hives rose from around 1,600 in 2008 to more than 7,400 by 2020, according to the London Beekeepers Association.

Supporters argue that urban bees benefit from a longer flowering season, since parks, gardens and even balconies bloom at different times across a city. Rooftop hives have appeared on buildings ranging from the Paris Opera House to hotels in New York.

Critics, however, warn that packing too many hives into a small area can actually harm wild bee populations by forcing them to compete for the same limited flowers. Some ecologists now recommend planting more flowers before adding more hives.

🔄 Negation Flip

The passage says more hives can harm wild bee populations. The question says they increase them. Same topic, same paragraph, same keywords — opposite direction. A student who scans for "wild bee populations", sees it, and answers TRUE has done four steps out of six correctly and still lost the mark.

Step 1 of 6 — press "▶ Play step 1" to begin. Use ← and → to move between steps.

Station 3 · 12 minutes

Map Drill — build it, then use it

Skimming is only worth doing if it produces something. That something is a map. Part A trains students to build the map in under two minutes; Part B proves the map works by making them locate six answers without reading a single full paragraph.

How to run it
Part A on the clock: 90 seconds, in pairs, one phone between two so they must argue about each choice. Do not let them read properly — walk round and say "you're at ground level, climb". Part B is individual and silent. Collect the Part B times: the pairs who skimmed properly in Part A will finish Part B in half the time of those who didn't, and that contrast is the whole lesson.
📄 The passage — read at cruising altitude only
Reading the Ice

A Every winter, snow falls on the high plateaus of Antarctica and Greenland, and almost none of it melts. Each year's snowfall is buried and compressed by the snow that follows, until it hardens into dense glacial ice. Drilling teams extract this ice in cylindrical sections roughly ten centimetres across, called cores, raising them to the surface metre by metre. The deepest such projects have reached more than three kilometres below the surface.

B What makes the ice valuable is what is trapped inside it. As snow compacts, it seals off pockets of the atmosphere, so each bubble is a physical sample of the air that existed when the snow fell. The ice itself carries further information: the ratio of oxygen isotopes in the frozen water varies with the temperature at which it condensed, allowing past temperatures to be reconstructed without any written record.

C Reading the sequence depends on counting layers. In regions with heavy snowfall, individual years can be distinguished visually, much as tree rings are, and counted downwards. Deeper in the core, the layers thin under pressure until they can no longer be separated by eye, and researchers must instead match chemical signals — the ash from a known volcanic eruption, for instance — against dated events elsewhere.

D The results reshaped climate science. Cores drilled at Vostok, and later at Dome C, showed that carbon dioxide concentrations and temperature had risen and fallen together across eight glacial cycles, spanning roughly 800,000 years. Crucially, they also showed that the concentrations reached during the twentieth century lay far outside the range of that entire period.

E The method has limits. Ice flows slowly downhill, so the deepest layers are stretched and folded, and near the bedrock the record can be scrambled beyond use. Nor is old ice evenly distributed: only a handful of sites are thought to preserve continuous ice older than a million years. Several international teams are now drilling in East Antarctica in the hope of recovering it.

Station 4 · 12 minutes

Keyword Lab

Students underline half the question and then wonder why scanning takes forever. A keyword is not "an important word" — it is a word you can physically search for. Part A cures over-marking. Part B fixes the deeper problem: the passage almost never uses your keyword, it uses a paraphrase of it.

How to run it
Do the first item of Part A on the big screen as a class and deliberately over-mark it — tap eight words — so the page tells you off, not them. The rule to write on the board: concrete keywords are for finding, idea keywords are for checking. You scan for the concrete one; you then read carefully around it to test the idea one. Part B is where bands 5 and 7 separate.
🎯 Part A — Mark only what you can search for
Tap the keywords. Two or three, no more.

Each statement refers to the Reading the Ice passage in Station 3.

🔁 Part B — The paraphrase problem
Your keyword will not be in the text. Its disguise will.

For each question phrase, find the wording the passage actually uses. This is the single highest-value habit in IELTS Reading — scanning fails when students search for the exact word instead of its meaning.

Station 5 · 12 minutes

Scan Sprint

Now the narrow skill on its own, under a clock. Scanning is dense reading over a small area — your eyes move slowly across every word, but only inside the paragraph the map already chose. Speed comes from the small area, never from moving your eyes faster.

How to run it
Demo first on the big screen: read the prompt aloud, then say "which paragraph — shout it" before anyone hunts. Only then reveal. Insist on the two-move habit: choose the paragraph, then scan it. Students who skip straight to hunting will beat the class on question one and lose badly by question five. For the pair race, one student holds the phone and reads prompts aloud while the other searches, then they swap.

Station 6 · 14 minutes

Trap Detector

Finding the sentence is not the same as answering the question. Examiners build False and Not Given options to reward exactly the student who scans well and then stops thinking. This station gives them a four-point check to run on every located sentence — and then punishes them for skipping it.

✅ The four-point check
Run this on the sentence you found — every time, in this order

Read the whole sentence, not just the keyword. Then compare it to the claim on four axes. If any one fails, the answer is not TRUE.

Check 1
Same subject?
Is the text talking about the same people, place or thing the claim names — or a different group that merely sounds similar?
Check 2
Same direction?
Rose or fell? Helped or harmed? Encouraged or discouraged? This is where most marks are lost.
Check 3
Same strength?
"Some" is not "all". "May" is not "will". "Suggests" is not "proves". A claim that is too strong is FALSE.
Check 4
Same scope?
True in one city is not true everywhere. True in 2020 is not true always. Watch for silently widened boundaries.
The rule that fixes NOT GIVEN
Students guess NOT GIVEN when they feel unsure. Give them the actual test: FALSE means the text contradicts the claim. NOT GIVEN means the text never addresses it at all. If you can point at a sentence and say "this says the opposite", it is FALSE. If you cannot point at any sentence, it is NOT GIVEN. Feeling uncertain is not evidence.
🪤 The four traps
🔄 Negation Flip
The claim states the exact opposite of the text, using the same keywords so it scans as a match.
text: harm → claim: increase
⚖️ Qualifier Swap
A hedge is quietly upgraded to an absolute. The topic matches perfectly; the strength does not.
text: some → claim: all
🗺️ Scope Shift
Something true of one place, group or period is stretched to cover everything.
text: in London → claim: worldwide
🔁 Reversed Cause
A and B are both present and both correct — but the arrow between them is turned around.
text: A causes B → claim: B causes A

Station 7 · 20 minutes + 8 to review

The Full Run

Six skills are not a strategy until they run together against a clock. One passage, thirteen questions, three question types, twenty minutes — the same question-per-minute rate as the real exam, in the same split-screen layout as computer-delivered IELTS. The page watches your pace and, afterwards, tells you exactly which question cost you the paper.

How to run it
Silent, individual, phones out, no talking — this is the only station that should feel like an exam. Two things to say before you start the clock: "the pace marker is your opponent, not the questions", and "if a question passes ninety seconds, flag it and move on." Then say nothing for twenty minutes. The debrief afterwards is where the teaching happens: go straight to the timing chart and find the tall red bar. That single question is almost always the reason a student runs out of time, and they will not have noticed it themselves.
The tactic almost nobody teaches
Questions 5–13 follow the order of the passage. Questions 1–4 do not. True/False/Not Given and sentence completion march downwards through the text, so the answer to Q7 is always below the answer to Q6 — which means every answer you find shrinks the search area for the next one. Matching-information questions (1–4) jump around, so save them for last, when you have already read most of the passage looking for everything else.

The Rediscovery of Roman Concrete

AThe dome of the Pantheon in Rome has stood for almost two thousand years. It contains no steel, spans forty-three metres, and remains the largest unreinforced concrete dome ever built. Roman harbour walls, poured directly into the sea two millennia ago, are still standing in the Bay of Naples. Modern reinforced concrete, by contrast, is frequently scheduled for major repair within fifty to a hundred years. That contrast has puzzled engineers for generations, and only in the last decade have laboratories begun to explain it.

BThe Roman recipe was not concrete as we understand the word. Instead of the finely ground Portland cement used today, Roman builders combined lime with pozzolana — a volcanic ash named after the town of Pozzuoli, near Naples — and, for structures built in the sea, mixed it with seawater rather than fresh water. The architect Vitruvius set down proportions for these mixtures in the first century BC, and his account survived, which is why the ingredients themselves were never really a mystery. What was mysterious was why they lasted so long.

CThe seawater turned out to be the first clue. Salt water is ruinous to modern concrete: it corrodes the steel embedded inside and splits the material apart from within, which is why coastal structures are among the most expensive to maintain. Roman marine concrete behaves in the opposite way. Analysis of cores drilled from ancient breakwaters showed that seawater percolating through the material had, over centuries, dissolved parts of the volcanic ash and allowed rare crystals to grow in the spaces left behind. The structures were not merely surviving the sea; they were being strengthened by it.

DMarie Jackson, a geologist at the University of Utah, identified the crystal responsible as aluminous tobermorite, a mineral that is extremely difficult to manufacture under laboratory conditions and yet had formed slowly and unaided inside Roman piers. Her work established that the reaction was still continuing long after the concrete had set — in some cases for two thousand years, a timescale no modern building material is designed around.

EA second explanation arrived from a different direction. A team led by Admir Masic at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology examined the small white lumps, known as lime clasts, scattered through Roman samples. These had long been dismissed as evidence of careless mixing. Masic's group argued instead that they were deliberate, produced by a technique called hot mixing in which highly reactive quicklime is added directly to the mixture. When the researchers deliberately cracked laboratory samples made this way and ran water through them, the cracks sealed themselves within two weeks. Identical samples made without the clasts never sealed at all.

FNone of this means the industry can simply revert to ancient practice. Roman concrete is weak in tension, sets far more slowly than modern mixes, and could not carry the loads that a contemporary bridge or tower demands. It cannot be reinforced with steel, because the very chemistry that protects the concrete is the chemistry that attacks the metal. Volcanic ash of the right composition is not available in most of the world either, and shipping it across oceans would cancel out much of the environmental argument for using it.

GWhat is genuinely being taken from the Romans is narrower and more useful: the principle that a material can be designed to repair itself, and that durability may matter more than initial strength. Cement production is estimated to account for around eight per cent of global carbon dioxide emissions, and a substantial share of that is spent replacing structures that failed early. A concrete that lasted three hundred years instead of eighty would reduce those emissions without a single change to the way cement is burned. Measured that way, the Pantheon is not a curiosity. It is a specification.

If you only have 20 minutes
