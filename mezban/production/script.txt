After years abroad, a young man finally came home to Bangladesh. His father had been waiting for this day.
He held his son close. His wife and daughter stood beside them, smiling. Their family was together again.
The father was a wealthy man, and he wanted to share his joy. He decided to hold a mezban, a great feast.
Cooks stirred enormous pots of rice. The family prepared beautiful invitations and sent them to rich neighbors and important people.
Under a white canopy, the tables were laid and the chairs were ready. There was room for hundreds of guests.
But the people who received the invitations had other plans. One man waved the messengers away at his gate.
An important official barely looked up from his desk. He said he couldn't come.
A businessman was busy with his phone. A wealthy couple said they had no time. Each had an excuse.
The messengers returned with their invitations still in their hands. Almost nobody had agreed to come.
The father and his family waited by the gate. They watched the road, hoping the guests would arrive.
The rice was ready. Steam rose from the pots, and the plates gleamed on the tables. But the chairs were empty.
Time passed. The feast was ready for everyone, but the invited guests couldn't be bothered to come.
The messengers explained what had happened. The father listened, then looked across his empty courtyard.
For a moment, his face fell. All this food, and nobody to share it with.
Then he looked beyond the gate. A tired rickshaw driver sat beside the road. Children searched for something they could eat.
Nearby stood a group of hijra. They weren't on anyone's guest list. The father looked at them, and his expression changed.
He opened the gate wide and spread his arms. Come in, he called. My son's home, and we're having a feast. Come and celebrate with us.
The people outside stared at him. They were used to being turned away from houses like this.
Do you mean us? someone asked. All of you, the father said. There's a place for you here.
His son smiled. The family stepped forward to welcome their new guests. Slowly, the people began to come through the gate.
Rickshaw drivers came in with children who lived on the streets. The hijra came too, still wondering if they were really welcome.
The father greeted them himself. His son offered water, and the children washed their hands.
Please, sit down, they said. The guests took their places. The family stayed beside them, talking and smiling.
Then the father picked up a serving spoon. He filled a plate for his son and another for an older guest.
He served a child, then his wife. His son took a spoon too and began to serve the guests.
There was plenty to go around. Plates passed from hand to hand. Nobody was sent away, and nobody had to eat outside.
Soon the courtyard was full of laughter. The father offered more rice, and his guests smiled back at him.
He stood with the hijra guests, laughing and talking. They were part of the celebration, just like everyone else.
The father and son sat down among the people they had welcomed. They shared the food and listened to each other's stories.
Women and men, young and old, rich and poor, people from Muslim backgrounds and Hindu backgrounds, all had a place at the feast.
At one table sat the family, rickshaw drivers, people who begged for a living, and hijra guests. They ate together, passing food and sharing their joy.
The father looked around the table. His son was home. His house was full. And the feast was finally what he had hoped it would be.
