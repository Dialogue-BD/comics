Culture in four rings

You can only see the outside ring. Every ring inside it has to be asked for — and then checked against three people, because one person’s answer is a story, not a culture.

  • Behaviour what is done? The outside ring, and the only one you can watch.
  • Values what is good or best? What they put first.
  • Beliefs what is true? What has to be true for that value to make sense.
  • Worldview what is real? The shape of the world those beliefs assume.

For the teacher

An eight-panel lesson, then five steps: about 45 minutes. The lesson is shown once and the browser remembers; “Show the lesson” brings it back for a new class. Its second panel is the one that matters — the same three values ranked two ways — and it is worth stopping on.

Each lesson panel is a picture that plays in short beats, with one line of caption under it. You do the talking: → or a tap on the picture moves it on one beat, ← steps it back, and only after the last beat does → turn the panel. The short bars at the foot of the screen jump to any beat, “Play again” replays the panel, and “Read it” opens the panel’s paragraph for a class that wants the words.

Every step is a short run of moments, and each moment asks for one thing: one line of instruction at the top, one thing on the screen, the bars at the bottom to show where you are. → moves on a moment and ← goes back one. A moment that asks the class to do something keeps Next closed until it is done, then Next glows. The bars jump back to anything already seen.

Listen is the language work. Each person is met, heard once with nothing to read, heard again while the class taps the words it catches, and heard a third time with the words lit as they are said — any underlined word opens its meaning. Only the first listen is required. Each person ends as one line on a board of three, and Check starts from that board: six sentences, one at a time, each asking only “all three, or only one?”

In Map each ring offers three sentences: the right one, one of the three traps — restatement, judgment, stereotype — and the sentence that belongs one ring deeper. The traps are worth more discussion than the right answers. Pairs work well on phones: one runs the audio, the other keeps notes on the printed field sheet.
