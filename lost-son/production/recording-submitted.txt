A father lived in a village with his two sons. The older son worked beside him. The younger son wanted a different life.
One day, the younger son approached his father. He was tired of waiting for his future to begin.
Father, he said, give me my share of the family property. I want to leave.
His brother heard him. The father looked at the young man, then quietly agreed to divide what he owned between his sons.
The younger son could hardly believe it. His father had given him what he had asked for.
The father opened his cupboard. There were papers to sign and money to hand over.
He signed the papers, then placed the money in his son’s hands.
The young man held it close, smiling. Now, he thought, I can live exactly as I please.
He packed his suitcase and left the family home.
With his money and his plans, he walked away from the village where he had grown up.
He climbed onto a bus. The green fields slipped past the window.
At the station, he bought a ticket for a train. Soon he was far from home.
The city was crowded, noisy, and full of possibilities.
He found a room in a comfortable hotel and looked out over the bright lights. Everything seemed to be waiting for him.
He bought new clothes and an expensive phone. For the first time, he could buy whatever caught his eye.
He made new friends. They ate in expensive restaurants, laughed together, and stayed out late.
There was always another meal to order, another bill to pay, another reason to spend.
Don’t worry, he told them. It’s on me. His friends smiled and raised their glasses.
For a while, it felt like freedom. Nobody told him when to come home or how to use his money.
But the money kept leaving his wallet. He spent it as if it would never run out.
Then the meals became quieter. He counted what was left, and his friends stopped coming around.
At last, he opened his wallet and found almost nothing inside.
Hard times came to the city. Food cost more, and work was difficult to find. He could no longer pay for his room.
His new phone was damaged. The people he had called his friends were nowhere to be found.
He stood beside a food stall, showing his empty pockets. The seller sent him away.
At the river, he watched men carrying heavy loads. He needed work. Any work.
Please, he said to a man at the fish market. I’ll do whatever you need.
The man gave him a job cleaning fish. He worked among the baskets and the mud, for very little money.
Day after day, he grew weaker. He had left home with so much. Now he couldn’t even feed himself.
Dogs ate the scraps beside the market. He watched them, holding his empty stomach.
At night, he pulled a sheet of plastic around himself and tried to sleep.
One morning, he finally came to his senses. He thought about his father’s house.
Even the people who work for my father have more than enough to eat, he thought. And here I am, starving.
He washed his face at a hand pump. He could no longer pretend that everything was fine.
I’ll go home, he decided. I’ll tell my father I’ve sinned against God and against him.
I’m not worthy to be called his son anymore. Perhaps he’ll let me work for him instead.
He set off with very little. Along the way, he took odd jobs in the fields.
Sometimes a truck gave him a ride. Often, he walked barefoot, carrying his shoes.
At last, the familiar fields came into view. Then he saw his father’s house.
His father was standing on the veranda, looking down the road.
Far away, a figure was coming toward the house. The father looked again.
The clothes were dirty. The shoulders were bent. But he knew that walk. It was his son.
He put down his cup without thinking about the spilled tea.
He gathered up his clothes and began to run.
He didn’t wait for his son to reach the gate. He ran out to meet him.
The young man dropped to his knees. His hands were empty. He had nothing to offer.
Before he could explain, his father reached him and wrapped both arms around him.
The father held his son’s head against his face. For a moment, neither of them said anything.
Then the son began. Father, I’ve sinned against God and against you. I’m no longer worthy to be called your son.
But his father was already calling to the people in the house. Help him wash. Bring him our best clothes.
The father helped him dress. He wasn’t being received as a stranger or a worker. He was being welcomed as a son.
Prepare a feast, the father said. We’re going to celebrate.
Soon, food was cooking in great pots. The courtyard filled with music and laughter.
My son was lost, the father said. Now he’s found. I thought I’d lost him forever, but he’s home.
Meanwhile, the older brother was coming back from the fields. He heard the music before he reached the house.
A boy ran out from the courtyard. Your brother’s home, he said. Your father’s having a feast because he’s come back safe.
The older brother’s face changed. A feast? For him? After everything he’s done?
He was angry and refused to go inside. So his father came out to speak with him.
All these years I’ve worked for you, the older son said. I’ve done everything you asked.
You never gave me a feast with my friends. But this son of yours wastes your money, comes home, and gets a celebration.
The father listened. My son, he said, you’re always with me. Everything I have is yours.
But we had to celebrate. Your brother was lost, and now he’s found. He was gone from us, and now he’s home.
Come in, my son. Come and be glad with us.
