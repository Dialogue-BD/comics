## The intended result

A learner should leave each unit able to identify a design feature, understand the corresponding language feature, predict an effect, and apply that idea to a fresh example through a small game. The visual and textual examples should both do real teaching.

The prototype contributes stronger subtype coverage and memorable interaction ideas. Our current activity contributes the studio and paper-workspace identities, the original visual word animations, several stronger literary examples, richer motion, source examples, and a tested learning shell.

The agreed scope is several short challenges in every unit. Plan for three complementary challenges: notice, manipulate, and transfer. Optional subtype comparisons add examples, while the fifteen-unit structure contains the scope. The fifteen-unit foundation remains the whole activity. Rewriting stays tap/drag/order/group/trim based; extended typed writing is not required.

## A consistent three-stage lesson

1. **See and name.** Start with one clear learning goal and the paired motion. Bring two or three useful subtypes into the main teaching surface. Each term has an actual visual/textual example, a short definition, and a feature the learner can tap or highlight. Select visual examples from the supplied guide; the full original page remains available for closer study. B1 wording comes first, with technical vocabulary explained in place.
2. **Change a choice.** Replace the generic binary before/after treatment where needed with two or three named states. The visual and written examples respond to the same choice. Keep the scenario and core information stable when testing one feature. Show the specific change and its effect; prompt the learner to notice before revealing the explanation.
3. **Play the bridge.** Give each unit three short, complementary challenges: notice a feature, manipulate it, and transfer it to a fresh example. The action should demonstrate the concept: grouping gathers ideas, cutting removes clutter, repainting changes diction, and alignment repairs a pattern. Recognition can occur during stage one, manipulation during the lab, and transfer in the final stage, so practice is distributed through the lesson. Feedback names the evidence and intended effect. Small substeps get inline cues; the bottom green coach marks a completed challenge. Worked versions remain available.

Optional examples deepen a unit without adding another obligatory lesson sequence. The choice of visual or written entry changes emphasis and ordering within this shared core, rather than creating duplicate curricula.

## Four pilots before a full rollout

**Diction:** identify a word-art meaning, repaint a scene through palette-based word-chip selection, then transfer the choice to a fresh scene. Give a short scene and an intended atmosphere, let the learner manipulate a small number of choices, then compare the wording and palette. The explanation should distinguish connotation, intensity, and information changes.

**Texture:** reuse the musical visual. Sort contrasting phrases, build a sound texture, then edit a fresh phrase for a target rhythm. The feedback points to particular repeated sounds or phrasing. Audio, where used, must actually demonstrate the current example; transcripts remain available.

**Proximity:** identify related details, upgrade grouping into physical sentence magnets, then transfer the grouping principle to a visual-caption task. The chosen groups become readable paragraph blocks and a corresponding visual grouping. Preserve the focus of the original scene and add a fresh transfer round.

**Simplicity:** spot redundancy, perform paper surgery with live before/after comparison, then choose a concise version of a fresh example. Removing a tile removes a visible distraction. Check that the action and purpose survive, and calculate the remaining text accurately.

These pilots cover four interaction families already supported by the app and make the pedagogical merge easy to evaluate. Line and Repetition follow next; Shape, Alignment, and Form retain their recent specialist examples while gaining targeted comparisons.

## Content and feedback rules

- Every vocabulary term has an example and a visible or audible feature. A decorative label alone is insufficient.
- A lab identifies what changed. If facts, tone, emphasis, or implication change as well, the explanation names that change.
- A challenge states its purpose before judging an answer. Feedback refers to evidence in the actual example.
- Subtext remains an inference. An open context can support more than one reading.
- Font associations, colour moods, and sound textures are presented through the chosen context. They are not universal rules.
- Preserve B1 brevity and the user's current five-part chiasm. Deeper subtype coverage sits in short optional comparisons.
- Biblical selections, adaptations, and stanza/structural teaching layouts stay accurately labelled and linked to their sources.
- Keep all navigation open. XP records practice; it does not certify mastery.

The source review found checks that should be rebuilt during adoption: the prototype pause game accepts any single break; its surgery counter starts at nine for an eleven-word sentence; subtext gets one fixed interpretation without adequate evidence; and the punctuation game conflates a particular balancing effect with the only correct punctuation. The interaction metaphors are reusable; their feedback needs to follow the intended learning outcome.
